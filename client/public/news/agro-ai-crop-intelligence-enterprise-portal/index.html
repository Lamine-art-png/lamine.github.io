AGRO-AI launches Crop Intelligence.

A new intelligence layer connects what is happening in the crop with what it means for the operation and the business—bringing field evidence, weather, irrigation, risks and next steps together with production, inventory, contracts, costs, prices and market context.

AGRO-AI•San Francisco, California• Tuesday, September 22, 2026 · 8:00 a.m. PT•20 minute read

Crop Intelligence is the broader crop decision layer inside the AGRO-AI Enterprise Portal. It connects two questions that agricultural teams usually have to answer in separate systems: what is happening to the crop and operation? and what does that mean commercially?

Weather changes. Irrigation systems generate telemetry. Field teams make observations. Images capture visible conditions. Operators leave notes. Sensors produce measurements. Equipment creates status and fault information. At the same time, production expectations change, inventory moves, contracts cover part of the crop, realizable prices move, freight and storage affect economics, currencies change, and physical or exchange-linked market observations arrive with different levels of freshness and authority.

Crop Intelligence brings those layers into one connected decision story. The operational side organizes observed evidence, exposes missing information, separates conflicts from conclusions, and surfaces crop, water and equipment signals. The commercial side is delivered through the Market Intelligence workspace, which structures positions, production, inventory, contracts, costs, prices, market observations and scenarios using deterministic economics. Together they help teams understand what deserves attention in the field and what that change can mean for exposure, margin and commercial decisions.

In brief
  • Bring relevant crop, field, weather, irrigation, equipment and operational evidence into a common intelligence layer when those sources are available.
  • Connect that operational picture with the Market Intelligence workspace: production, inventory, contracts, costs, realizable prices, freight, storage, FX and governed market observations.
  • Surface important signals, missing information, source conflicts and next evidence actions instead of flattening everything into a single score or unsupported forecast.
  • Use deterministic economics for exposure, break-even, projected revenue, margin and scenario deltas; model synthesis is grounded on those facts rather than inventing the arithmetic.
  • Connect crop understanding to the broader Enterprise Portal workflow, including field work, recommendations, scheduling, execution, verification, reports and governed decision history.
  • Keep consequential authority with people and controlled workflows. Crop Intelligence does not silently operate equipment, execute trades, or turn weak evidence into a definitive diagnosis or market prediction.

More data is rarely the missing ingredient.

Agricultural teams already work with a large amount of information. The challenge is that useful context is distributed across tools that were built for different jobs. A weather service can tell a team what happened in the atmosphere. A controller can record irrigation activity. A sensor can report a measurement. A field note can describe a symptom that no sensor understands. An image can preserve visual evidence. A spreadsheet can contain historical context. Meanwhile contracts may live in another system, production estimates in another file, physical-market prices in messages or reports, and currency, freight or storage assumptions somewhere else again.

Each source can be useful on its own. The work becomes difficult when a team has to reconstruct the relationship between them every time a decision is needed. Which observation belongs to which field? Is the sensor reading recent enough to matter? Does the weather context support the same interpretation? Is the controller history complete? Did a field team already inspect the area? How much of expected production is contracted? What volume remains exposed? Is the current realizable price based on a governed source or a manual observation? What happens to margin if price, yield or FX changes? What information is still missing?

Crop Intelligence is built around those questions. It starts with context rather than a generic dashboard. The AGRO-AI operational intelligence layer can assemble workspace and field information, source readiness, field intelligence, exceptions, decision context, reports and available evidence into a tenant-scoped grounding packet. In parallel, Market Intelligence maintains a tenant-scoped commercial position for the crop or commodity and computes commercial truth with fixed-precision arithmetic. The two layers answer different parts of the same management problem.

The question is not only “What is happening in the crop?” It is also “What does it mean for the operation, the economics and the next decision?”

One operating view, without pretending every signal means the same thing.

A common mistake in agricultural software is to make heterogeneous information look uniform. A sensor measurement, an operator observation, a model-derived result and a missing source can all end up displayed as if they have equivalent authority. That may create a clean interface, but it creates a weak decision record.

AGRO-AI takes a different approach. Crop Intelligence is built on evidence and commercial models that preserve source type, timing, confidence, quality, freshness and provenance. The system can keep an observation as an observation, a deterministic calculation as a derived finding, a conflict as a conflict, and an unknown as an unknown. The interface can then make the operating picture simpler without erasing the distinctions that matter.

Observed

Field observations, image observations, operator notes, sensor records and other source evidence remain tied to their provenance.

Derived
What can be calculated or synthesized

Derived findings can be connected to evidence and deterministic checks rather than presented as unexplained certainty.

Unknown
What the team still needs

Missing measurements, unresolved conflicts and weak coverage remain visible so the next step can be evidence collection instead of guesswork.

This distinction is especially important for crop health. Visible stress can have multiple causes. A single image can be useful evidence without being a complete diagnosis. A sensor can indicate a condition without explaining the biological response. A field observation can be accurate while still requiring follow-up. Crop Intelligence is designed to preserve those boundaries.

What Crop Intelligence can bring together.

The exact view depends on the sources available to a customer workspace. AGRO-AI does not claim that every operation has every source, and it does not fabricate live coverage when a provider or dataset is unavailable. When evidence exists, the broader intelligence context can combine multiple operating domains around the selected workspace and field.

Crop and field context

Crop type, field or block identity, region, field observations, image observations, operator notes and other field-scoped records.

Weather and environmental context

Weather observations and relevant environmental information when connected or otherwise available in the workspace.

Irrigation and water evidence

Telemetry, meter readings, moisture or sensor context, recent irrigation history and other water-related records that can support a grounded view.

Equipment and controller context

Controller, valve, pump, pressure, flow, fault and equipment observations when those sources are connected and eligible for the selected workflow.

Uploaded and operational records

Customer-owned files and structured records can contribute context while retaining source identity and explicit quality state.

Readiness, exceptions and decisions

Source readiness, missing coverage, exceptions, workbench context, reports and prior operational state can help explain what is ready and what still needs review.

Commercial position

Expected production, carry inventory, production cost, freight, storage, current realizable price and reporting currency form the deterministic economic baseline.

Contracts and market observations

Contracted quantities and prices, manual or governed physical-market observations, FX reference data and configured benchmark context can support exposure and scenario analysis.

The important point is not the length of the source list. It is the ability to bring evidence into a common operating context while keeping the source boundaries intact. A customer with three reliable sources can have a stronger decision foundation than a customer with twelve poorly mapped ones.

One intelligence layer across crop, operations and market context.

Inside AGRO-AI, Crop Intelligence does not live as an isolated feature with its own disconnected data model. It is the umbrella decision layer connecting crop and field understanding with water, equipment, assurance, reporting and the commercial Market Intelligence workspace. That architecture matters because crop conditions are rarely independent of either the operation or the economics around the crop.

A crop-health question may depend on a field observation. The follow-up may require an image. A water-related signal may add context. An equipment fault can change how the irrigation record should be interpreted. The same operation may also need to understand expected production, contract coverage, exposed volume, current realizable price, costs and margin. Assurance may need the evidence later. A manager may need the same event represented in a report. Keeping those workflows connected reduces the amount of manual translation between systems.

From crop condition to commercial exposure.

For many agricultural businesses, understanding the crop is only half the decision. The same team may need to know how much production is expected, how much is already contracted, what quantity remains exposed, what the current realizable price is, how freight and storage affect economics, and what happens to margin under different market or production scenarios.

That is the role of Market Intelligence inside Crop Intelligence. The Market Intelligence workspace maintains commercial positions by crop or commodity, season, geography and reporting currency. It can track expected production and carry inventory, production and inventory costs, contracted quantities and prices, current realizable prices, freight and storage economics, and market observations with explicit source state and provenance.

The commercial engine is intentionally deterministic. Fixed-precision arithmetic—not a language model—calculates contracted and uncontracted volume, weighted contract price, locked and exposed revenue, break-even, projected cost and margin, quantity conversions, FX conversions and scenario deltas. Natural-language synthesis sits on top of those facts and must remain numerically grounded.

The product also treats market-data authority explicitly. AGRO-AI currently supports governed ECB reference FX, USDA AMS MyMarketNews for supported U.S. positions when the required API access is configured, and customer-owned physical-market observations stored as manual evidence. Physical, hybrid and exchange-linked market structures are supported in the domain model, but AGRO-AI does not pretend that an exchange feed is live unless the licensed provider, credentials and freshness controls actually exist.

Market scenarios are not predictions. They let a team ask structured “what if” questions against its own position—for example, how exposure or margin changes under a different price, yield or FX assumption—without representing the scenario as a forecast or a trading instruction. Crop Intelligence does not execute trades or connect to brokerage execution.

Surface what matters. Keep uncertainty visible.

Operational intelligence is useful only when the user can tell why something is being surfaced. Crop Intelligence can organize observed evidence and deterministic findings, detect relevant conflicts, expose unknowns and cap confidence when the evidence is weak.

For example, when conflicting source readings exist, the system can put the item into conflict review rather than quietly choosing one reading. If there is no relevant crop-health evidence, confidence is constrained and the next step can be a request for field-scoped evidence. If required context is missing, that missing information can remain explicit.

That behavior is intentional. Agricultural operations contain noisy, delayed and incomplete information. A useful intelligence system should degrade honestly. It should be able to say that the evidence is limited, that a source is stale, that two records disagree, or that an inspection is needed before a stronger conclusion is appropriate.

Designed for the real rhythm of agricultural teams.

A crop condition does not become valuable because it appears on a dashboard. It becomes valuable when the right person understands it soon enough to respond appropriately. That means the product has to work with the rhythm of field teams, managers, advisors, operations staff and enterprise stakeholders rather than forcing every user into the same workflow.

Crop Intelligence is therefore designed around a common operating view. A field team may contribute observations. An operations manager may focus on the priority signals. A crop advisor may inspect the underlying evidence. A water manager may care about irrigation context. An enterprise leader may need a concise view of risk and readiness across multiple workspaces. The same underlying evidence can support different roles without requiring each team to rebuild the context independently.

For larger organizations, that consistency matters. The challenge at scale is often not the absence of expertise. It is keeping the expertise connected to current information across many fields, regions, systems and people. A shared evidence layer helps the organization preserve context as responsibility moves from one person or team to another.

Global by design.

Crop Intelligence is not organized around a single country, commodity or farming system. The operational context model is based on organization, workspace, field, crop, region, evidence, source and operating state, while Market Intelligence adds commodity position, season, geography, reporting currency and market structure. That makes the product useful as a common layer across different geographies while allowing agronomic, operational and commercial interpretation to remain specific to the crop, environment and market reality.

That distinction matters for global agriculture. A field in California, an orchard in New Zealand, a row-crop operation in Brazil and a specialty-crop operation elsewhere can have very different weather patterns, equipment, data availability, labor practices and operating constraints. A global product should not hide those differences behind generic assumptions.

AGRO-AI therefore treats local context and source quality as part of the intelligence problem. The platform can carry crop and region context, identify missing information, and keep unsupported claims out of the user experience. Where customer-owned data or configured providers are available, they can participate in the evidence graph. Where they are not, the product should remain explicit about the limitation.

How Crop Intelligence fits with Field Intelligence.

Field Intelligence and Crop Intelligence address different moments in the same operating loop. Field Intelligence is built around capturing what is happening in the field: observations, voice, images, notes, evidence and linked follow-up. Crop Intelligence is built around understanding crop-related evidence in context and helping the team see what matters across those signals.

A field observation can become evidence for Crop Intelligence. An image can contribute visual context. An operator note can explain what a sensor does not capture. Crop Intelligence can then surface the relevant crop-health evidence, expose unknowns, and recommend inspection or additional data collection when the current picture is incomplete.

That connection is important because agricultural intelligence should not start and end in a dashboard. The strongest operating record links what was seen, what was understood, what the team decided, what work followed and what was verified afterward.

How Crop Intelligence connects field, water, equipment and market intelligence.

Crop condition, water status, equipment state and commercial position are related but distinct domains. AGRO-AI keeps their specialized logic separate while connecting the resulting evidence and decisions. Water intelligence can work with telemetry, weather, sensors and meter readings. Equipment intelligence can work with telemetry, flow, pressure, controller and field observations. Crop-health intelligence focuses on crop, leaf, canopy, pest, disease, lesion, stress, symptom, plant and fruit context from relevant evidence. Market Intelligence focuses on the commercial position: production, inventory, contracts, costs, realizable prices, market observations, FX and scenarios.

The shared operating model lets these domains inform one another without collapsing them into one opaque answer. A crop-health signal can be reviewed next to water context. An equipment issue can explain a telemetry anomaly. A change in expected production can alter commercial exposure. A market move can change the economics of a crop without changing the crop's biological state. A report can preserve the evidence chain. The team can then decide which interpretation and action is appropriate.

What the system deliberately does not do.

Crop Intelligence is decision support, not invisible authority.

The crop-focused specialist does not autonomously approve actions, schedule irrigation, execute equipment commands, submit official records or change controller state. It does not convert a single observation into a definitive diagnosis when the evidence does not support one. The market layer does not execute trades, connect to brokerage execution, present scenarios as forecasts, or invent live prices and exchange data. Neither layer fabricates telemetry, crop state, weather, acreage, water use, market coverage, source authority or customer facts. Human authority and controlled workflows remain explicit.

These boundaries are part of the product, not a limitation hidden behind the interface. We believe serious agricultural software should make it easier to act with evidence while preserving the difference between assistance and authority.

A better interface starts with a better evidence model.

The visible experience of Crop Intelligence is intentionally simple: one operating view, clear priorities, source-aware evidence and understandable next steps. The backend work required to make that simplicity credible is more demanding.

The intelligence context builder is tenant-scoped and workspace-aware. It can assemble readiness state, field intelligence, exceptions, decision workbench context, report context, source content and evidence records. Sensitive credential-like fields are removed from intelligence context. Evidence citations can preserve source type, source identity, tenant, workspace and trace metadata. Source text is bounded so the system does not blindly ingest unlimited context. Commercial profiles can also control source limits and scope.

The grounding layer can preserve observed facts, deterministic scientific checks, conflicts, unknowns and confidence. Specialist domains select the evidence relevant to their task. For crop health, that includes field observations, image observations, operator notes and sensor evidence, along with relevant phenology checks when available. If the evidence is weak, the system reduces confidence rather than filling the gap with invented precision.

The Market Intelligence backend follows the same truth-first principle through a separate commercial model. Financial truth is computed with fixed-precision numerics; source observations carry provider, source state, freshness, timestamps, units, currency and licensing metadata; missing FX, over-contracting or incomplete cost information can suppress totals instead of publishing a misleading partial result. The AI layer can explain the position, but it does not replace the deterministic arithmetic.

That architecture is the reason the front end can be clean. The user should not have to inspect every backend mechanism to benefit from it, but the mechanism should still be there when trust, review or auditability requires it.

Frequently asked questions.

What is Crop Intelligence?

Crop Intelligence is the broader crop decision layer inside the AGRO-AI Enterprise Portal. It connects crop and field evidence with operational intelligence and the Market Intelligence commercial workspace so teams can understand what is happening and what it means for the business.

Does Crop Intelligence include Market Intelligence?

Yes. Market Intelligence is the commercial decision surface of Crop Intelligence. It structures production, inventory, contracts, costs, realizable prices, freight, storage, FX, governed market observations and deterministic scenarios.

Does it predict crop futures or tell me what to trade?

No. Market Intelligence supports exchange-linked, physical and hybrid market structures, but scenarios are simulations rather than predictions or trading instructions. AGRO-AI does not execute trades, connect to brokerage execution, or claim live exchange data without a licensed and configured source.

What market data can it use today?

AGRO-AI supports governed ECB reference FX, USDA AMS MyMarketNews for supported U.S. positions when the required API access is configured, and customer-owned physical-market observations recorded as manual evidence. Other sources remain explicit as unavailable or not configured until the required provider access exists.

What data does it need?

There is no single mandatory source list. Operational sources can include field observations, images, operator notes, sensors, weather, irrigation telemetry, controller or equipment context and uploaded records. Commercial context can include production, inventory, contracts, costs, prices, currencies, freight, storage and governed market observations.

Does it diagnose crop disease automatically?

Crop Intelligence can organize crop-health evidence and surface relevant symptoms or risk context, but it is designed to preserve uncertainty and evidence boundaries. A limited observation remains limited evidence. Stronger conclusions require appropriate supporting context and human judgment.

Can it control irrigation equipment?

The crop-focused specialist layer is side-effect-free. It does not directly approve, schedule or execute equipment actions. AGRO-AI has separate governed workflows for operational decisions and connected systems.

Can enterprises access these capabilities through an API?

Yes. The AGRO-AI Platform API is the developer-facing surface for teams that want to integrate AGRO-AI intelligence and operational workflows into their own systems.

Is Crop Intelligence available globally?

The product is designed for global agricultural operations. Its core context model is based on organization, workspace, crop, field, region, sources and evidence rather than a single geography.
